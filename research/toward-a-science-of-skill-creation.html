Toward a Science of Skill Creation

From instructions to reproducible capabilities

DD Tech Research · Working paper · November 2026

From prompt writing to capability engineering

Artificial intelligence systems increasingly rely on specialized skills to perform complex tasks. Yet the process of creating those skills remains largely experimental: write instructions, try them, observe the results, and revise.

There is an opportunity to make this process more systematic.

We propose treating skill creation as an engineering and research discipline: the systematic design, construction, testing, and improvement of reusable capabilities. We call this proposed field Skill Science.

The central problem

A capable AI model does not automatically possess the procedural expertise needed to complete every task reliably. It may understand a subject but fail to apply that knowledge. It may follow instructions without recognizing when they are relevant. It may produce a convincing result without verifying whether the result is correct.

A useful skill must address more than knowledge. It must connect knowledge to appropriate action.

Existing disciplines provide a foundation

Several established fields offer useful starting points:

  • Cognitive task analysis examines how experts perform complex tasks.
  • Cognitive apprenticeship studies how expertise can be communicated and developed.
  • Deliberate practice investigates structured improvement through feedback.
  • Design science research develops and evaluates purposeful artifacts.
  • Software engineering provides methods for testing, verification, and maintenance.

These disciplines are relevant, but they do not yet constitute a unified methodology for engineering reusable AI skills.

A proposed development cycle

  1. Discover: Identify a task, capability gap, or recurring failure.
  2. Analyze: Examine the task, its conditions, decisions, and required knowledge.
  3. Model: Represent the capability as a structured process.
  4. Construct: Build the instructions, references, resources, and tools.
  5. Activate: Establish when and how the capability should be selected.
  6. Execute: Apply the skill to representative tasks.
  7. Evaluate: Measure performance against defined criteria.
  8. Evolve: Revise the skill using observed failures and new evidence.
This cycle is a research proposal, not an established scientific standard.

What should be measured?

A skill should be evaluated through observable performance. We propose measuring task completion, depth, specificity, practical usefulness, evidence handling, reliability, completeness, and efficiency.

The evaluation should distinguish between a model's baseline performance and its performance when using the specialized skill. Otherwise, we cannot determine whether the skill actually contributed to the improvement.

The research opportunity

Skill creation needs more than better prompt writing. It needs repeatable experiments, explicit models of capability, comparative testing, and methods for identifying why a skill succeeds or fails.

The longer-term objective is to understand how reusable expertise can be engineered across different models, environments, and tasks.

A skill should not be considered successful because its instructions look sophisticated. It should be considered successful when it produces a measurable improvement in real task performance.

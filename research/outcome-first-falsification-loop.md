# The Outcome-First Falsification Loop

*A practical method for turning an interesting idea into evidence without getting trapped in endless preparation.*

Ideas are cheap to generate. The difficult part is finding out which ones survive contact with reality.

A useful research workflow should therefore begin with the outcome, not with an elaborate plan. It should make the next action small enough to perform, the result clear enough to inspect, and the conclusion modest enough to revise.

This article introduces a working method developed in the VOIDD research project. It is a proposed practice, not a scientifically validated universal method.

## The loop

> **COALESCE → COMPRESS → QUEST**

- **Coalesce:** identify the single objective that matters now.
- **Compress:** reduce the work to one next action.
- **Quest:** perform that action and record a measurable result.

The loop repeats only after the result has been inspected.

## 1. State the objective

Describe the outcome in concrete terms. “Research AI agents” is a topic, not an objective. “Determine whether an agent can complete a three-step task in a changing environment” is closer to something testable.

A useful objective tells us what would count as a result.

## 2. Compress the next action

Choose the smallest action that can produce relevant evidence. Avoid building infrastructure before knowing whether the question is worth pursuing.

If the question is whether a landing page attracts interest, publish a minimal page and measure visits or sign-ups. Do not begin by building a complex analytics platform.

## 3. Make a prediction

Write down what you expect to observe and what result would count against the idea. This prevents every outcome from being reinterpreted as success after the fact.

A hypothesis becomes useful when it risks being wrong.

## 4. Inspect the result

Keep the evidence separate from interpretation. A simple record can use five labels:

- **Observed:** directly measured or inspected.
- **User-stated:** reported by a person.
- **Inferred:** follows from available evidence but was not directly observed.
- **Hypothesis:** a proposed explanation or prediction.
- **Unknown:** not yet established.

This makes uncertainty visible instead of hiding it inside confident prose.

## 5. Update, stop, or expand

After inspection, choose one of three moves:

- **Update:** revise the hypothesis or method.
- **Stop:** the result does not justify more effort.
- **Expand:** the result supports a larger test.

The important rule is that activity alone is not progress. Progress requires an inspectable change in knowledge, capability, or outcome.

## A compact experiment record

| Field | Example |
|---|---|
| Objective | Test whether a page attracts local business owners |
| Hypothesis | A clear offer will produce qualified inquiries |
| Next action | Publish one-page offer and contact 20 prospects |
| Measure | Visits, replies, qualified inquiries |
| Result | Record actual numbers and observations |
| Decision | Update, stop, or expand |

The numbers in this example are illustrative, not reported results.

## Why keep it small?

Large plans can create the feeling of progress before anything has been tested. Small experiments expose assumptions earlier, reduce wasted effort, and create evidence that can guide the next decision.

The method is not an argument against deep research. It is a way to connect research to contact with the world. Background investigation should improve the experiment, not indefinitely postpone it.

**Open question:** can this loop help individuals and AI collaborators consistently convert open-ended exploration into useful, verifiable outcomes?
